CAD-Acid

An acid test for CAD kernels.

Part of the wonky project — a Rust CAD kernel that speaks Onshape's FeatureScript and build123d, aims to be exact and introspective, refuses rather than guesses, and checks its work against Onshape.

The idea: one frozen model, forty-eight small rooms

The catalog is one model, but not one shape. It's a grid: each zone gets its own 250 mm cell (deliberately a power of two in metres, so the grid itself introduces no rounding), keeps its geometry within about 90 mm of its own local origin, and never touches its neighbours. One zone (a large-coordinate test living at x = 131,072 mm) gets an oversized cell of its own so its scale doesn't leak into the others.

Every zone is described twice, from the same construction parameters: once as an Onshape FeatureScript function, once as a build123d module driving OCCT. These aren't two renderings of "the same shape" — they're two independent implementations of the same written spec, which is the whole point: if the FeatureScript twin and the build123d twin quietly diverge, that's a bug in the catalog, not a kernel difference to shrug off.

And crucially, a zone is never checked by looking at it. There is no image diffing anywhere in CAD-Acid. Instead each zone is checked against hard facts: closed-form volumes and areas where they exist, canonical topology counts (faces, edges, genus, singular points), tight bounding boxes, and specific targeted distances between named points, edges and bodies. A kernel can render a beautiful picture of the wrong shape; CAD-Acid doesn't care what it looks like, only what it measures as.

The knackpunkt: sometimes the test has to say what's right

Most of geometry has one right answer. Some of it doesn't — not because the geometry is ambiguous, but because different kernels are built to answer a slightly different question, and both answers are legitimate. That's the knackpunkt (German for the crux, the sticking point), and CAD-Acid handles it head-on: every zone declares its accepted outcomes separately per kernel class, and separately lists the outcomes that look like a pass but aren't.

There are two kernel classes in play. Exact kernels (wonky's Rust core) preserve exact incidence — a gap of a billionth of a millimetre is real geometry to them, and merging it or dropping a sliver to make the model "cleaner" is exactly the failure CAD-Acid is built to catch. Tolerance kernels — Onshape's Parasolid (its documented booleanDefaultTolerance is 10⁻⁵ m = 0.01 mm) and OCCT (Precision::Confusion, 10⁻⁷ mm) — can legitimately heal gaps within documented tolerances. Catalog v1 incorrectly interpreted the Onshape tolerance as 10⁻⁵ mm. An accepted tolerance branch must be explicit in the catalog, preserve valid material and remain consistent across rotations and translations; catalog v2 must fix the AC40 contract rather than presenting documented healing as a kernel defect.

Three examples from the catalog show what this looks like in practice:

  • A sub-resolution gap. One zone places two boxes so close together that, once the interpreter's floating-point arithmetic has run, they're separated by about 9.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ mm — a nominal 2⁻³⁰ mm before the millimetre-to-metre conversion rounds it. An exact kernel must report two bodies with that exact gap between them; it is never allowed to decide the gap is "basically zero." A tolerance kernel may legitimately report two bodies (gap kept, or measured as zero) or a single merged body — both are accepted — but whichever it picks, it has to pick the same thing under every rotation and translation of the model. The named silent-wrong outcome: an exact kernel silently closing the gap, or any kernel taking a different branch in different orientations.
  • A tangent contact. Another zone places two cylinders of equal radius so their axes are exactly the sum of the two radii apart — proven by the actual floating-point values the FeatureScript interpreter computes, not by decimal intuition. The cylinders touch along a single line and nothing more: no overlap, no gap. The accepted outcomes are two untouched bodies, or a named refusal because the union along a single line of contact isn't a manifold solid. What's silent-wrong: reporting one fused body and claiming it's a valid, manifold solid (it structurally can't be, at a line of tangency), or quietly turning the contact into a small lens or bridge of extra material to avoid the awkward topology.
  • A horn torus / pinch point. One zone revolves a circle whose centre sits exactly as far from the revolve axis as its own radius — so the circle just touches the axis at one point. Revolving that gives a horn torus: a torus pinched shut into a single singular point rather than a torus with a hole. The accepted outcomes are a solid that honestly represents that one singular point (and whose own validity check accepts its own singular topology), or a named refusal that the revolved profile is tangent to the axis. The named silent-wrong outcome is the sneaky one: a ring torus with a tiny but positive hole — which passes every volume, area and bounding-box check, because the hole can be made arbitrarily small — is still wrong, because its topology (a genus-1 surface with no singular point) is a different shape from a pinched horn torus, no matter how close the numbers get.

How a zone gets checked

A zone's grade never comes from a screenshot. It comes from a stack of independent checks, applied per zone and per kernel:

  1. Closed forms, where they exist. Volumes and areas for prisms and revolves are re-derived by Green's theorem in exact rational arithmetic; axis-aligned box booleans are re-derived by exact coordinate compression; the harder curved cases (a Steinmetz solid, a T-junction, a tangent-chain fillet, a helix) are re-derived by 50-digit numerical quadrature. All of this runs in a standalone checker, independent of the catalog's own hand-written expressions, and it currently passes 1,561 separate checks against the 48 zones — with 17 deliberately planted mistakes in scratch copies of the catalog all caught. 44 of the 48 zones have a closed form; 2 more have closed-form bounds but no single shared answer (see below); the remaining 2 accept only a named refusal.
  2. Cross-comparison, where no closed form exists. Two blend zones use blending conventions that legitimately differ between kernels, so there is no single "true" volume to check against. For those, the frozen Onshape reference is the primary reference, accepted only if OCCT's independent volume agrees with it within a declared band; if they disagree beyond that band, the zone is marked disputed and no kernel earns a point for it that round. The parts of those zones that do have closed forms — bounding geometry, fillet setbacks, spring lines — are still checked exactly.
  3. Validity and a STEP round trip. Every geometric result has to pass the kernel's own boundary-representation validity check, and it's then exported to STEP and re-imported through OCCT, which re-measures the zone's topology and every closed-form quantity all over again. A kernel that produces the right numbers but an invalid solid, or one that loses a feature in export (a tiny hole that a STEP writer silently drops, say) fails here even if its own reported volume looked fine. One narrow exception is predeclared for a sliver so thin that OCCT's own tolerance can't represent it at all — there, the STEP file is only checked structurally, not re-measured.
  4. Four metamorphic variants of every zone. Each zone is built four times, under transforms that are applied to the construction inputs (never to the finished result), and every variant has to agree on the same accepted outcome:
    • V0 — identity. The zone exactly as authored.
    • V1 — a large translation. A shift by roughly 65.5, −32.8 and 16.4 metres, chosen to be exact binary fractions so the move itself adds no new rounding, but far enough out that floating-point spacing widens noticeably (at that distance, adjacent representable positions are already about 1.4×10⁻¹¹ mm apart) — a stress test for whether sub-resolution facts still survive at scale.
    • V2 — exact 90° rotations. A 90° turn about one axis, then another about a second, built from exact axis vectors rather than sine and cosine, so this variant tests handling of "nice" angles without introducing its own floating-point noise.
    • V3 — a skew rotation. A genuinely inexact 0.1-radian turn about the axis (1, 2, 3) through a fixed point. This is the one variant whose numbers really don't come out clean, and it's deliberate: it tests whether a kernel decides tangency, contact and gaps from the construction's own frame — the same way it would under V0 — rather than by re-measuring rounded world coordinates after the rotation, which would silently merge a real sub-resolution gap that survived every other variant.

A zone only passes if every one of its four variants passes and produces the same accepted outcome; a kernel that's right under V0 but merges a gap under V3 has revealed that its earlier "right" answer wasn't actually decided from the geometry.

Scoring: pass, refuse, or be wrong out loud

Every zone, for every kernel, lands in one reported verdict; the six scored buckets below are joined by DISPUTED for documented catalog errors:

VerdictPointsMeaning
PASS1Every variant gives the same accepted geometric outcome, within the zone's tolerance, with valid topology and a valid STEP round trip.
REFUSED_EXPECTED1Every variant gives the same named refusal, and that refusal is one of the zone's accepted categories.
REFUSED0A named refusal where geometry was actually expected — or any refusal because the operation simply isn't implemented yet. That second case always scores zero, even in zones that do accept some refusal, specifically so a kernel can't "pass" a zone by refusing to try.
ERROR0A crash, a panic, a timeout, or any error that isn't a named, categorised refusal.
WRONG0, and counted separately in redA result that succeeded but isn't one of the accepted outcomes — split into geometry (numbers out of tolerance), validity (an invalid solid or a failed STEP round trip), topology (wrong canonical counts), or metamorphic (accepted outcomes that differ between the four variants).
NOT_RUN0, and listed by nameThe zone was not executed; it stays in the denominator.
DISPUTED0The frozen catalog or a twin needs correction; the zone is suspended for every kernel until catalog v2, while remaining in the denominator.

Two rules make this stricter than a plain pass rate. First, the precedence across a zone's four variants is WRONG, then ERROR, then REFUSED, then REFUSED_EXPECTED/PASS — so a kernel that passes three variants and merges a gap on the fourth doesn't get partial credit, it gets flagged as metamorphically wrong. Second, "wrong is loud": a plausible-looking success that isn't on the predeclared accepted list is never averaged away into a lower score, it's counted and reported separately, because a kernel that fails loudly is far more useful than one that fails quietly and looks fine.

Side note: where the name comes from

"Acid test" is a good name to borrow, and it comes from an actual family of tests with a specific history worth getting right rather than gesturing at.

Acid1, originally called the Box Acid Test, was written by Todd Fahrner, developed in October 1998, and folded into the CSS1 test suite on January 26, 1999. It checked a single page of box-model rendering — margins, padding, borders, floats — against a reference image, at a time when browsers disagreed wildly about how CSS1's box model was supposed to work.

Acid2 followed in 2005: developed by Håkon Wium Lie and Ian Hickson (Hickson wrote the final version), and released by the Web Standards Project on April 13, 2005. Rendered correctly, the page draws a simple smiley face with "Hello World!" underneath it, and the nose turns blue when you hover over it — a test result you could recognise from across the room, not just a percentage. It probed HTML markup, CSS 2.1 positioning and the box model, PNG alpha transparency, data URIs, and generated content. Safari 2.0.2 became the first officially released browser to pass it, on October 31, 2005.

Acid3 came in 2008: written by Ian Hickson, developed from April 2007 and released March 3, 2008. It moved away from a single rendered picture and toward 100 numbered subtests covering the DOM, DOM2 events, CSS3 selectors, table manipulation, SVG/SMIL, and ECMAScript — reporting a live percentage counter instead of a face. Development builds of Opera and of WebKit (the engine behind Safari) both reported 100/100 within weeks, on March 26, 2008; Safari 4, the first officially shipped browser version to hit 100/100, followed later.

The analogy to CAD-Acid holds up in the ways that matter: a small, fixed page (or model) that many independent engines are run against side by side, with a result specific enough to be worth bragging about or worth fixing. It breaks down in one important way, though. The browser Acid tests exist because there's supposed to be exactly one correct rendering of a given page — the whole point is that every standards-compliant engine converges on the same smiley face. CAD-Acid explicitly doesn't assume that: it predeclares that an exact kernel and a tolerance kernel are allowed to give different correct answers to the same zone, and some of those answers are only "correct" because the test says so, not because there's a spec mandating one true rendering. And where the Acid tests are graded by eye or by an on-page counter, CAD-Acid is graded without ever looking at a picture — because for a CAD kernel, unlike a browser, a plausible-looking wrong answer is the specific failure mode worth testing for.
